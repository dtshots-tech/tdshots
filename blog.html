Process & Approach

How We Approach Street Automotive Photography in Liverpool

By Deano April 2026 8 min read

Most car photography happens in controlled settings — booked studios, closed roads, perfect lighting. T&DSHOTS works the opposite way. We head out into Liverpool with no booking, no model on the calendar, and no guarantee there's anything worth shooting. That unpredictability is the whole point.

When Tom and I started this, the idea was straightforward: find interesting cars in the wild, photograph them properly, and see what happens. No permission forms, no studio time, no paying for access. Just us, the kit, and whatever Liverpool had parked outside that day. What we quickly realised is that the street produces shots that a controlled environment never could. The grime on a wheel arch against wet tarmac. A Lamborghini parked outside a chip shop. A classic 911 sitting in a Tesco car park like it doesn't know how rare it is.

The grime on a wheel arch against wet tarmac tells a story that a studio backdrop never will. That context is everything to us.

The Scout

Before we shoot, we scout. Liverpool has particular areas where interesting cars tend to congregate — certain car parks, certain postcodes, certain times of day. We've spent a lot of hours just driving around, phone in the cupholder, noting what we saw and when. That intelligence is now one of our most valuable assets. We know roughly where to go on a Sunday morning versus a Friday evening, and we know which spots are worth returning to after a night's rain because the reflections off the wet ground are something else.

We always go in pairs. Partly because it's more efficient — one of us can hold position on a car while the other fetches the printer — but mainly because it's better creatively. Having a second set of eyes changes what you notice. Tom will be focused on the lens and I'll be reading the environment: watching the light shift, clocking whether anyone is walking toward the car, spotting whether there's a better angle ten metres further back.

Reading the Light

Liverpool's light is inconsistent, which sounds like a complaint but is actually one of our biggest advantages. An overcast sky in October gives you the most even, flattering light you'll ever shoot in. The harsh summer midday sun is almost unusable for anything except silhouettes. We've learned to plan around the forecast rather than fight it. Golden hour in Liverpool hits differently when the Mersey is in the background — and it lasts longer than you'd think because the river bounces light back.

We don't use external lighting rigs. Everything is natural, available light. Part of that is practical — we move fast and don't want to carry gear — but part of it is a deliberate aesthetic choice. We want our photos to look like Liverpool, not like a render.

The Approach

When we find a car worth shooting, we don't set up a tripod and spend twenty minutes composing. We work quickly. Tom fires off a lot of frames in a short window, trying angles and distances fast, because the situation can change — an owner can appear, a van can park in front, the light can shift. The editing process then does a lot of work to pull the best frame out of that run.

After the shoot, if the car is still there, we print on the spot and leave the photo on the windscreen. That's the part people find most surprising about what we do. The owner comes back to their car and there's a professional-quality print of their vehicle sitting on the glass. No invoice, no catch. Just the shot. If they like it and want more, our details are on the back.

Behind the Idea

Why We Print on the Spot and Leave Photos on Windscreens

By Deano March 2026 6 min read

The windscreen print idea sounds almost too simple when you explain it out loud. We photograph a car, we print it, we put the print on the windscreen, and we walk away. But that simplicity is the whole reason it works — and the reactions we get when owners find their photos tell us we've stumbled onto something genuinely different.

The original inspiration came from thinking about what car photography actually is for. Most automotive photography exists to sell something — an ad campaign, a magazine feature, a dealer listing. The car owner themselves rarely gets to see their vehicle photographed the way it deserves. They might take a phone snap in a car park, or have a friend with a camera do something on a Sunday afternoon. But a properly composed, properly lit photograph of their specific car, in a context that actually looks like their life? That almost never happens unless they commission it. And most people don't think to commission it.

We wanted to close the gap between the car culture we were documenting and the people who actually live it every day.

The Practical Side

Making it work required sorting out a portable printing setup that was good enough to actually be worth leaving on someone's windscreen. A blurry, banded print on thin paper would do more damage than good. We needed something that looked like a proper photograph — good colour, good sharpness, a finish that felt like it was worth keeping. Once we had that sorted, the logistics became straightforward: shoot, select the best frame, print while still on location, attach to the windscreen, and move on.

We leave a small card on the back with our details and a note pointing people to the website if they want the digital file or more prints. That's the only commercial element of the whole interaction. The print itself is a gift. No obligation, no pressure.

What Actually Happens

We don't often see the moment an owner finds their print — we're usually gone by then. But occasionally we're nearby when it happens, or people contact us afterwards. The reactions tend to fall into one of two categories. Some people are genuinely moved by it — they didn't expect it, they can't quite believe someone photographed their car, and the fact that it's sitting there as a physical print makes it feel real in a way a digital image on a phone never would. Other people are just delighted — they share it, they post it, they want more. Both of those responses are exactly what we hoped for.

The model also works as a form of marketing that doesn't feel like marketing. The print travels. It ends up on a dashboard, on a fridge, in someone's living room. Every time someone sees it, they see the T&DSHOTS name. Word of mouth in car communities moves quickly, and a physical artefact carries more weight than any social media post.

Gear & Setup

Our Kit: What Tom Shoots With and Why It Works for Street

By Tom February 2026 7 min read

Street automotive photography has specific demands that a lot of camera gear isn't really built for. You need to move fast. You need to handle wildly varying light conditions within the same shoot. You can't ask the car to wait while you change lenses. The kit I use for T&DSHOTS has been refined by working out in the field what actually gets the shots and what slows you down.

I'm not going to name specific models here because gear changes and what matters more than the specific camera body is the approach behind the choices. Everything we use is portable, weather-resistant where possible, and optimised for fast acquisition rather than studio precision. We're not shooting tethered. We're shooting handheld in car parks at 7am with whatever the sky gives us.

Lenses Over Bodies

If there's one principle that drives all of our gear decisions, it's that glass matters more than bodies. A good lens on a decent body will produce better results than a mediocre lens on an expensive body, every time. For automotive work, the focal lengths we reach for most are in the mid-telephoto range — they compress the car against the background in a way that flatters the proportions, and they let you work from a respectful distance so the shot doesn't look like you were standing on the bumper.

Wide angles have their place — front three-quarter detail shots, wheel arches close-up, that kind of thing — but our signature look, the one that makes our car sets feel cinematic rather than documentary, comes from that compression. It makes a hatchback look like a sports car. It makes a classic look heroic rather than dusty.

Glass matters more than bodies. A good lens on a decent body will produce better results than a mediocre lens on an expensive body, every time.

Handling the Light

Liverpool is not exactly a sunlit paradise. We shoot in conditions ranging from hard winter fog to full summer sun, sometimes on the same day. The key is not fighting the light — it's reading it and adapting. On grey days, we open up and shoot wide, letting the flat soft light do what it naturally does for metallic paint. On bright days, we look for shade, or we shoot with the sun behind the car to get that rim-light effect that makes the body lines pop.

We edit everything in a consistent style — slightly cooled highlights, lifted shadows, and a grade that keeps skin tones neutral even though there are no people in our shots. The consistency across the car sets is deliberate. When you browse our gallery, you should feel like you're looking at one body of work rather than a collection of individual experiments.

The Print Setup

The portable printer is as important as the camera, because the whole T&DSHOTS concept doesn't work without it. We've tested a number of options over time, and the requirements are strict — print quality that looks professional, speed that means we can produce a print before the owner comes back, and a form factor that fits in a bag without making the whole operation impractical. The chemistry of getting a good street photograph is one thing. The chemistry of getting a good print from a mobile setup in a car park is a different problem entirely, and one we've spent a lot of time solving.

Liverpool's Best Hidden Spots for Automotive Photography

By Deano January 2026 6 min read

Liverpool gets photographed a lot, but most of that photography focuses on the same handful of landmarks. The Three Graces, the docks, the cathedrals. We've been out in this city long enough to know where the less obvious spots are — places where the combination of architecture, light, and surface texture does something interesting to a car that you couldn't replicate anywhere else.

We're not going to give away every location we use — partly because some of them work precisely because they're not crowded with other photographers, and partly because the joy of scouting is in finding your own spots. But we can talk about the kinds of places we look for, and give a sense of why Liverpool produces such interesting automotive photography when you get beyond the obvious angles.

Industrial Architecture

Liverpool has a huge amount of industrial heritage, and a lot of it is either being repurposed or sitting quietly unused in areas most tourists never reach. Old warehouse districts, former dock infrastructure, railway architecture — all of it creates a backdrop that has texture, scale, and visual interest without competing with the car. A red brick Victorian warehouse behind a white supercar creates a contrast that neither one achieves alone. We return to industrial areas more than anywhere else because they give us that combination of monumentality and grit.

The light in these areas is also different from open streets. Narrow passages create shaft-lighting effects that you'd pay a lot of money to replicate in a studio. Wide open former dock spaces give you that cinematic shallow depth of field against an infinity of building. You learn to read these environments and understand what time of day makes each one sing.

Liverpool has a huge amount of industrial heritage, and a lot of it produces backdrops that have texture, scale, and visual interest without competing with the car itself.

Unexpected Residential Streets

Some of our favourite T&DSHOTS sets have come from residential streets in postcodes you wouldn't associate with interesting cars. Liverpool's car culture is spread more evenly across the city than people assume — the interesting vehicles aren't clustered in one area. A Georgian terrace in one of the older residential districts can produce a backdrop that's simultaneously grand and deeply local, which is hard to manufacture and impossible to fake.

The thing about residential spots is that you're working with real context. The car belongs there. It lives on that street. That relationship between vehicle and environment is what we're always trying to capture, and it's most authentic when the environment is genuinely where the car exists rather than a location chosen for its visual properties alone.

The Mersey and the Waterfront

The obvious locations exist for a reason. The Liverpool waterfront is genuinely one of the most dramatic settings in the UK for photography, and that applies to automotive work as much as anything else. The trick is not shooting the obvious composition — car parked in front of the Liver Building, shot from the front, every tourist angle covered. The waterfront becomes interesting when you use it as light source rather than backdrop, when you shoot toward the city rather than toward the river, when you work the edges and find the angles that aren't on any photography guide.

We use the waterfront most in the late afternoon when the sun is hitting the river and bouncing back at city-level. That reflected light is softer than direct sun and more interesting than overcast flat light. It's a narrow window — maybe forty minutes on a good day — but the shots from that window are some of the best we've produced.
